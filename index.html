Malaysia orders in

RM 11.9 billionspent on food delivery in 2024

Food delivery took off in the years Malaysia's eating places were closing. Where we eat out, what a meal costs and what we order have all moved since.

By Justin Wong, published October 2026

Malaysia's food and drink businesses earned RM 9,742 million from online sales in 2023, 10.8% more than in 2021 (Department of Statistics Malaysia, DOSM). No Malaysian agency counts the delivery orders themselves.

We follow the change through five things that are counted: where the eating places are, what Malaysians spend on delivery, what a meal out costs, the money and jobs in the trade, and the food on offer. Eating places here are the restaurants, food stalls, cafes, drink stalls and caterers that DOSM counts in its Economic Census.

Kuala Lumpur has three times Sabah's eating places, per person

The Klang Valley has its fair share of eating places, and Sabah falls short.

Malaysia had 136,453 eating places in 2022, about one for every 240 people. Kuala Lumpur has 706 for every 100,000 people and Putrajaya 210, so the two federal cities rank first and last among the sixteen states and federal territories.

Darker states have more eating places for their people. Kuala Lumpur leads with 706 for every 100,000, and Sabah has 231.

Selangor, Kuala Lumpur and Putrajaya, the Klang Valley, hold 38,720 of Malaysia's 136,453 eating places. That is 28.4%, close to their 27.9% share of the people.

Across the South China Sea the balance tips the other way. Sabah, Sarawak and Labuan hold 18.3% of Malaysians and only 13.8% of the eating places.

Selangor has more eating places than any other state, 24,625. Spread over 7.1 million people, that is 349 for every 100,000, thirteenth of sixteen.

OpenStreetMap, a free world map that volunteers edit, records where each outlet stands. It holds 26,744 Malaysian restaurants, cafes and fast-food outlets, about one in five of the census count.

The dots merge into one patch over the Klang Valley, where Selangor and Kuala Lumpur hold 36.7% of the mapped eating places. A thinner line follows the west coast south to Johor. Sabah and Sarawak are mostly blank, with dots where the towns are and little between them.

Malaysians spent more on food delivery in 2024

Food is near the top of what Malaysians buy online, and Grab takes two-thirds of delivery spending.

In 2024, 81.0% of Malaysia's internet users bought something online, according to the Malaysian Communications and Multimedia Commission (MCMC). Food and health essentials came second only to clothing. Three apps carry the meals ordered to the door, so an eating place can now reach customers who never walk in.

Each side of the chart counts its own group of shoppers. The left counts internet users who paid without cash online, and the right those who paid without cash in a shop. Of the shop group, 90.1% bought food and health essentials, against 73.4% of the online group. For the other seven things on the chart, the online share is the higher one.

Grab took 66% of the money Malaysians spent on food delivery in 2024, up from 65% the year before. ShopeeFood rose from 5% to 9%, and foodpanda fell from 30% to 25%. Spending grew from US$2.4 billion to US$2.6 billion, or about RM 11.0 billion to RM 11.9 billion, so foodpanda took less money in a bigger market. The latest regional count, for 2025, has Southeast Asia's delivery spending up 18% to US$22.7 billion, about RM 97.2 billion.

A meal out costs more than it did

The rise has been steepest in the towns and in Putrajaya.

Since January 2015 the price of a meal out has risen 42.9%, against 26.0% for food cooked at home. A meal out here means food or drink bought ready to eat from a restaurant, stall or cafe. Delivery apps sell meals from those same kitchens, though DOSM keeps no separate price for the delivered ones.

Every line starts at 100 in January 2015, so 142.9 means prices have risen 42.9%. Everything Malaysians buy rose 24.3% over the same years, so food cooked at home kept close to prices overall. The price of a meal out rose less than 1% in the year after the Movement Control Order (MCO) stopped dining in on 18 March 2020. The two food lines moved together until 2022, and the gap between them has widened every year since.

DOSM prices towns and the country separately, and it counts eating out together with hotel stays in one spending group, eating out and hotels. That group rose 24.0% in towns between January 2019 and August 2026 against 15.9% in the country. Food and drink has the second widest gap, so both groups that cover food sit at the top. Phone and internet is the only group that got cheaper in both, down 5.3% in towns and 1.4% in the country.

Eating out and hotels cost 36.7% more in Putrajaya in August 2026 than in January 2019, the most of the sixteen states, and 13.5% more in Kelantan, the least. Putrajaya's prices rose 18.4% in 2022 alone, the biggest one-year rise for any state from 2011 to 2025. Ten of the sixteen states had their fastest year in 2022 or 2023.

Fewer kitchens, more money

Employment grew as the count fell, and the Klang Valley holds two in five jobs.

The Economic Census gives three counts of the trade, for 2010, 2015 and 2022. Between the last two the number of eating places fell by nearly one in five, so what Malaysians pay for meals, at the counter or through an app, now goes to fewer kitchens. The money the eating places took, the value of everything they sold, rose by half, and the number of people working in them by a fifth.

Malaysia's eating places employed 1,079,843 people in 2022, and 292,295 of them worked in Selangor. Kuala Lumpur adds 153,179 and Putrajaya 5,459, so the Klang Valley holds two in five of the jobs. Sabah has 3.4 million residents and 56,284 people working in its eating places, fewer than Pulau Pinang's 73,319 on half the population.

Malaysia counted 167,490 eating places in 2015 and 136,453 in 2022, 31,037 fewer. Over the same seven years the money they took rose from RM 66.4 billion to RM 99.0 billion, and the people working in them from 891,616 to 1,079,843. The count is still 5,883 above 2010, so the fall erased most of the gain made between 2010 and 2015. Businesses in the trade reported RM 8,932 million of online sales to DOSM for 2022, about 9% of the trade's RM 99.0 billion.

Food services, the restaurants and food stalls, took RM 401,000 for every eating place in 2015 and RM 773,000 in 2022, 1.9 times as much before allowing for higher prices. Beverage services, the cafes and drink stalls, went from RM 397,000 to RM 716,000, while event catering barely moved, from RM 357,000 to RM 415,000. A meal out cost 18.0% more in 2022 than in 2015, so at 2015 prices food services still took about 1.6 times as much.

Western and fast food leads the menu in most states

Kedah, Pahang, Negeri Sembilan and Sarawak are the exceptions.

A delivery app can only offer what nearby kitchens cook. OpenStreetMap lets volunteers record the cuisine each outlet serves, and 13,160 of the 26,744 mapped outlets name one. We sort them into ten kinds of food, from Malaysian and Chinese to coffee and drinks.

Western and fast food, Chinese food and Malaysian food make up 59.1% of the 13,160 outlets that name a cuisine. Japanese and Korean food, at 542 outlets, edges past Indian at 533.

Western and fast food is the biggest group in eight of the twelve states. Chinese food leads in Sarawak, at 29.5%, and Malaysian food in Kedah, Pahang and Negeri Sembilan. In Kedah 44.4% of the outlets that name a cuisine serve Malaysian food, almost three times its share of Western and fast food. Negeri Sembilan's lead is only two outlets and Pulau Pinang's six, so both are close to a tie.

Malaysians pay more to eat from fewer kitchens

Ordering in grew while the trade shrank to fewer, busier eating places.

The riders who carry Malaysia's orders now come under the Gig Workers Act 2025, in force since 31 March 2026. It covers about 1.64 million gig workers, people paid for each job they take.

How the measures are worked out

  • Eating places for every 100,000 people: a state's 2022 count of eating places divided by its 2022 population, times 100,000.
  • Shares of eating places and people: a group of states' total divided by Malaysia's, 136,453 eating places and 32.7 million people in 2022.
  • Price index from January 2015: each month's index divided by its January 2015 value, times 100.
  • Price change since January 2019: the August 2026 index divided by the January 2019 index, minus one, as a percentage. The gap between town and country is the town change minus the country change.
  • Change over the year, by state: a year's average index against the year before's.
  • Money taken: the census's gross output, the value of everything eating places sold in the year. Money taken for every eating place is that value divided by the number of eating places.
  • At 2015 prices: the 2022 figure divided by 1.180, the rise in the yearly average price of a meal out from 2015 to 2022.
  • Delivery spending in ringgit: the US dollar figure times Bank Negara Malaysia's average noon middle rate for that year, RM 4.57 to the US dollar in 2023 and in 2024 and RM 4.28 in 2025.
  • Online sales against money taken: RM 8,932 million of online sales divided by the RM 98,959 million that eating places took in 2022.
  • Mapped outlets by state: each OpenStreetMap outlet placed in the state whose boundary holds it. Outlets that fall outside every boundary are left out.
  • Kinds of food: each outlet goes to one of ten groups, by the first of its cuisine tags that matches a group. Shares are of the outlets that name a cuisine.
  • One plate is 100 outlets, rounded to the nearest plate. One square on the delivery chart is one per cent of the money spent.
